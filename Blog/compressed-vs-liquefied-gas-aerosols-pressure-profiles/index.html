Compressed Gas vs Liquefied Gas Aerosols: Pressure Profiles & Spray Behaviour

A compressed gas aerosol uses a non-condensable gas — most commonly nitrogen or carbon dioxide — stored entirely in the vapour phase at high initial pressure. Unlike systems that rely on a liquefied propellant, the pressure inside a compressed gas aerosol decreases progressively with each actuation because there is no liquid reservoir to replenish expelled gas. This fundamental distinction governs every aspect of spray performance, formulation compatibility, and storage behaviour, and it is the first engineering decision any aerosol product developer must make. Understanding how each propellant system behaves across its pressure-temperature envelope is essential for designing a product that performs consistently, complies with applicable regulations, and reaches market with predictable shelf life.

What Is a Compressed Gas Aerosol?

A compressed gas aerosol stores its propellant entirely as a gas within the can headspace, with no phase change occurring during storage or use. The gas is charged at a defined pressure at the point of filling, and this charge represents the system's entire pressure reservoir. Common compressed gas propellants include nitrogen (N₂), carbon dioxide (CO₂), and compressed air in certain specialised applications. Each is stored in its supercritical or gaseous state at ambient temperatures, meaning there is no liquid propellant phase present inside the can at any point in the product's life cycle.

The operating principle is straightforward but has significant implications: as product is dispensed and the headspace volume expands, gas pressure drops according to ideal gas law behaviour. The relationship between volume and pressure means that a can at 50% fill will deliver noticeably lower spray velocity than it did when full, all else being equal. This pressure decay is not a defect — it is an intrinsic characteristic of the technology that formulators must account for in product design.

Nitrogen as a Compressed Gas Propellant

Nitrogen is the most widely used compressed gas propellant due to its chemical inertness, lack of global warming potential (GWP = 0), non-flammability, and complete absence of solvency effects on organic formulations. It is the standard choice for food-contact aerosols, pharmaceutical applications, and products where hydrocarbon solvency would compromise formulation stability. Because nitrogen has negligible solubility in most liquid formulations, it does not dissolve into the product concentrate, which simplifies pressure calculation during filling. As documented by PubChem, nitrogen is a diatomic gas with near-ideal behaviour across the pressure and temperature ranges relevant to aerosol applications.

Carbon Dioxide as a Compressed Gas Propellant

Carbon dioxide occupies a unique position: at the pressures and temperatures encountered in aerosol manufacturing, CO₂ may exist at or near its critical point, exhibiting more complex phase behaviour than nitrogen. It is moderately soluble in many organic solvents and aqueous systems, which means dissolved CO₂ can influence formulation pH and contribute additional pressure as it evolves from solution. These factors require careful control during filling and stability testing. CO₂ is widely used in food aerosol applications — particularly beverage and whipped cream systems — where its interaction with the product is by design rather than a liability.

  • Nitrogen: GWP = 0, non-flammable, inert, no solvency — preferred for food, pharma, and sensitive formulations
  • CO₂: Moderate solubility in water and organics, can affect pH, higher initial pressure potential
  • Compressed air: Used in limited industrial applications; oxygen content creates oxidation and flammability risks in many formulations
  • Filling is performed at a defined charge pressure; no liquid propellant phase is introduced to the can

How Liquefied Gas Propellants Work

Liquefied gas propellants — most commonly blends of liquefied petroleum gas (LPG: butane, isobutane, and propane) and dimethyl ether (DME) — are stored inside the aerosol can as a liquid in equilibrium with their own vapour. This liquid-vapour coexistence is the defining thermodynamic feature of these systems. As long as liquid propellant remains in the can, the vapour pressure above it is governed by the propellant's own vapour pressure curve at the prevailing temperature — not by how much propellant has been used. This is the fundamental engineering advantage of liquefied gas propellants: they provide consistent propellant pressure throughout most of the can's useful life, declining only once the liquid phase is exhausted.

The vapour pressure of LPG blends is well-characterised by Raoult's Law for ideal mixtures, where each component contributes to total vapour pressure in proportion to its mole fraction in the liquid phase. The British Aerosol Manufacturers' Association (BAMA) and the European Aerosol Federation provide guidance on blending practices, with butane (vapour pressure approximately 2.0 bar at 20°C), isobutane (approximately 3.0 bar at 20°C), and propane (approximately 7.4 bar at 20°C) each contributing different pressure levels depending on their proportion in the blend. Formulators select the blend composition to achieve a target vapour pressure appropriate for the product and its container rating.

DME as a Co-Propellant and Solvent

Dimethyl ether occupies a dual role in aerosol formulation: it functions simultaneously as a propellant and as a powerful co-solvent. Its vapour pressure at 20°C is approximately 4.3 bar, which positions it within the LPG range, but its water miscibility and strong solvency for many organic compounds make it unique among propellant choices. DME is particularly valuable in formulations where complete solubilisation of active ingredients into the propellant phase is required, and in water-based systems where it acts as a bridge between aqueous and non-polar components. However, its solvency also means it can cause swelling or degradation in certain elastomers and polymer materials, requiring careful material compatibility testing of all valve components.

Key Insight Liquefied gas propellants exhibit temperature-dependent vapour pressure behaviour governed by the propellant's liquid-vapour equilibrium, not by the quantity of propellant remaining. This means spray performance stays consistent from first to near-last actuation — a critical advantage for consumer products where spray uniformity is a purchase-driving attribute.
  • LPG blends (butane/isobutane/propane): customisable vapour pressure by blend ratio; flammable; widely used in personal care, industrial, and household aerosols
  • DME: dual propellant and co-solvent; water-miscible; excellent solvency; requires polymer compatibility verification
  • HFCs and HFOs: non-flammable fluorinated propellants; used in pharmaceutical MDIs, foam applications, and specialty products
  • Pressure remains nearly constant until liquid phase depletion — typically the last 5–10% of product life

Pressure Profiles Compared: The Core Technical Distinction

The most practically consequential difference between compressed gas and liquefied gas aerosols is their pressure-depletion profile. In a compressed gas system, pressure follows a declining curve closely approximated by the ideal gas law: as product is dispensed and headspace volume increases, pressure decreases proportionally. A can that starts at 8 bar gauge might deliver only 4 bar gauge when half-empty, resulting in measurably different spray output. In a liquefied gas system, pressure remains stable across the depletion curve because the liquid-vapour equilibrium maintains a constant vapour pressure at constant temperature — pressure drops only when the last of the liquid propellant evaporates.

This distinction has profound consequences for product design. Consumer products with consistent spray requirements — hairsprays, deodorant body sprays, paint aerosols — universally favour liquefied gas propellants precisely because uniform pressure delivery translates to uniform spray output across the can's useful life. Compressed gas systems, by contrast, are accepted — or even preferred — in applications where declining pressure is tolerable, or where the non-flammable and inert nature of nitrogen or CO₂ is required by regulation or formulation constraint.

Property Compressed Gas (N₂/CO₂) Liquefied Gas (LPG/DME)
Pressure profile during use Declining (follows ideal gas law) Near-constant (liquid-vapour equilibrium)
Spray consistency Decreases toward depletion Consistent throughout most of can life
Flammability Non-flammable (N₂, CO₂) Flammable (LPG/DME); some HFCs non-flammable
GWP Zero (N₂); low (CO₂) Low (LPG, DME); varies (HFC/HFO)
Solvency effect on formulation Negligible (N₂); moderate (CO₂) Significant (DME, LPG); varies by propellant
Temperature sensitivity of pressure Linear with absolute temperature Follows vapour pressure curve (non-linear, often steeper)
Can rating required Higher initial pressure specification Determined by blend vapour pressure at 50°C
Typical applications Food-grade, pharmaceutical, aqueous, sensitive formulations Personal care, household, industrial, paints

It is worth emphasising that the vapour pressure of LPG-based systems at elevated temperatures can rise more steeply than that of a compressed gas system at equivalent initial pressure. The NIST WebBook thermodynamic database provides vapour pressure data for individual propellant components across the relevant temperature ranges — essential reference data for any aerosol engineer designing systems for hot-climate distribution.

Spray Behaviour & Performance Characteristics

The propellant system choice directly shapes every aspect of spray performance — from initial spray velocity and droplet size distribution to the quality of atomisation at near-empty. In a liquefied gas system, the propellant flash-evaporates as it exits the valve, producing the energy that atomises the product concentrate into fine droplets. The rapid phase change from liquid to vapour at the nozzle is a significant contributor to spray quality in LPG and DME systems. As detailed in our guide to aerosol propellants explained, this flash evaporation mechanism is thermodynamically distinct from the mechanical expansion that occurs in compressed gas systems.

In a compressed gas system, the liquid product is pushed out of the can by gas pressure alone, without any flash evaporation from the propellant. The atomisation energy must therefore come entirely from the mechanical energy of gas expansion and the design of the actuator/nozzle system. This typically results in a wetter spray — larger droplet sizes at comparable pressures — which makes nozzle selection and orifice geometry particularly critical in compressed gas aerosol product development, as explored in detail in our aerosol technology and formulations resource.

Rule of Thumb Because compressed gas aerosols lose pressure throughout their life, the actuator and nozzle must be engineered to deliver acceptable spray performance at the minimum pressure that will be present at near-empty — typically the most demanding design constraint for compressed gas system development.

Droplet Size and Particle Distribution

Droplet size in aerosol products is a critical performance and regulatory parameter, particularly in pharmaceutical and pesticide aerosols. As documented in peer-reviewed aerosol science literature available via PubMed, liquefied gas propellants typically generate finer droplets with a narrower size distribution than compressed gas systems at equivalent initial pressure, because flash evaporation provides a powerful atomisation mechanism that compressed gas systems cannot replicate. For applications where fine particle fraction is a regulatory specification — such as metered-dose inhalers — this difference is decisive and is one reason why pharmaceutical aerosols almost universally use liquefied fluorinated propellants (HFAs) rather than nitrogen or CO₂.

For non-pharmaceutical applications where droplet size is a consumer experience parameter rather than a regulatory threshold, the differences can be managed through actuator engineering. Specialised mechanical-breakup (MB) actuators designed for compressed gas systems use turbulent chamber geometry and specific orifice configurations to compensate for the absence of flash evaporation, producing finer droplets than would result from a simple orifice at equivalent pressure.

Selection Criteria for Aerosol Formulators and Engineers

Choosing between a compressed gas and liquefied gas propellant system is a multi-dimensional engineering decision that must be made early in product development, as it affects formulation chemistry, filling line configuration, container and valve selection, regulatory compliance, and end-user experience simultaneously. There is no universally superior system — each has domains in which it provides clear technical advantages, and domains where it imposes constraints that must be managed through careful engineering.

The choice begins with the formulation itself. If the active ingredient or carrier system is incompatible with the solvency of LPG or DME — for example, certain biologically active materials, aqueous-dominant formulations, or oxidation-sensitive compounds — a nitrogen-based compressed gas system may be the only viable propellant option. Conversely, if spray consistency is paramount and the formulation is compatible with LPG, the engineering case for liquefied gas is strong. Our how aerosol cans work guide provides a useful foundation for understanding how these propellant choices interact with the rest of the aerosol system.

Key Decision Factors

  • Flammability requirement: If non-flammability is mandatory (food processing environments, certain medical settings, aircraft), compressed N₂ or CO₂ is required
  • Spray consistency requirement: Products where uniform spray output is a core performance or consumer experience requirement strongly favour liquefied gas
  • Formulation compatibility: Aqueous systems without organic co-solvents are generally better served by nitrogen, which has no solvency interaction
  • Regulatory market: Some markets impose flammability restrictions that dictate propellant choice; compressed gas systems simplify transport classification
  • Container pressure specification: Compressed gas systems require containers rated for higher initial charge pressures; cost and availability implications must be assessed
  • Environmental profile: Both system types can achieve low GWP; nitrogen is definitively zero-GWP; LPG and DME also have low GWP values compared to legacy HFC propellants

Regulatory & Safety Considerations

Both compressed gas and liquefied gas aerosol systems are subject to comprehensive regulatory oversight governing their design, testing, labelling, transport, and storage. The National Fire Protection Association's NFPA 30B standard — the primary US code for aerosol storage — classifies aerosol products into three levels based on flammability, with Level 1 (non-flammable, typically compressed gas systems) receiving the most permissive warehouse storage requirements. LPG-based aerosols, by contrast, typically fall into Level 2 or Level 3 classification, requiring sprinkler systems and dedicated storage zone configurations. For export markets, the EU Aerosol Dispensers Directive 75/324/EEC and its amended requirements govern pressure testing thresholds and labelling for all aerosol systems placed on the European market, regardless of propellant type.

Transport classification is a significant practical consideration. Compressed gas aerosols using nitrogen or CO₂ are generally classified as non-flammable compressed gases under UN hazmat regulations (UN 1950, Aerosols, non-flammable), which carries less restrictive transport requirements than flammable aerosol classifications. LPG-based aerosols are classified as flammable aerosols, requiring compliance with IATA Dangerous Goods Regulations for air transport, IMDG Code for sea transport, and ADR regulations for road transport within Europe. This difference in transport classification can have meaningful implications for product logistics strategy, particularly for brands targeting international distribution.

Both system types require hot water bath testing per ISO 11568 or equivalent national standards to verify that cans can safely contain their charged pressure at elevated temperature — a standard quality release test for all filled aerosol products. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the main difference between compressed gas and liquefied gas aerosols?
Compressed gas aerosols store propellant entirely in the gas phase at high initial pressure, so internal pressure drops progressively as product is dispensed. Liquefied gas aerosols store propellant as a liquid in equilibrium with its own vapor, maintaining near-constant pressure throughout most of the product's life because liquid continuously evaporates to replace expelled vapor.
Why do compressed gas aerosols lose spray force toward the end of the can?
Because compressed gas aerosols contain a fixed quantity of gas with no liquid reservoir to replenish vapor, the internal pressure decreases with every actuation. As the headspace volume increases and gas quantity decreases, the driving force behind spray delivery weakens, resulting in progressively softer spray output near depletion.
Which propellants are used in compressed gas aerosols?
The most common propellants for compressed gas aerosol systems are nitrogen (N₂) and carbon dioxide (CO₂). Nitrogen is chemically inert, has no GWP, and is the preferred choice for food-contact and sensitive formulations. CO₂ offers higher initial pressure but can influence formulation pH and is often used in food carbonation and specific beverage aerosol applications.
What is the constant pressure advantage of liquefied gas propellants?
Liquefied gas propellants — such as LPG blends (butane, isobutane, propane) and dimethyl ether (DME) — maintain a constant vapor pressure determined by temperature rather than the volume of gas remaining. This means spray performance stays consistent from the first actuation to near-empty, which is essential for products where uniform delivery is a performance requirement.
Are compressed gas aerosols safer than liquefied gas aerosols?
Safety classifications differ by propellant type, not strictly by the gas vs. liquid phase distinction. Nitrogen and CO₂ compressed gas aerosols are non-flammable and present no flammability hazard during storage or use. LPG-based liquefied gas aerosols are classified as flammable and require compliance with NFPA 30B warehouse storage classifications and other regulatory controls. Both systems are safe when properly designed and handled within their respective engineering specifications.
How does temperature affect compressed gas versus liquefied gas aerosol pressure?
Compressed gas aerosols follow ideal gas law behaviour — pressure increases approximately linearly with temperature (in absolute Kelvin), so a can stored in a hot environment shows a proportional pressure rise. Liquefied gas aerosols follow vapour pressure curves that also rise with temperature, but typically more steeply in the relevant temperature range, making thermal management especially critical for warehouse storage and transport compliance.
When should a formulator choose a compressed gas aerosol over a liquefied gas system?
Compressed gas systems are preferred when non-flammability is a regulatory or market requirement, when the formulation is incompatible with hydrocarbon or DME solvency (e.g., aqueous-only systems), when the product is food-grade, or when the application tolerates a declining pressure profile across the can's life. They are also favoured in pharmaceutical and medical aerosol contexts where propellant-formulation interaction must be minimised.

Aerosol Dip Tube Design: Depletion & Spray Consistency

By Global Aerosols Team · October 12, 2026 · 14 min read · Aerosol Technology & Engineering

Aerosol dip tube design determines how completely a can empties and how steady its spray stays from the first actuation to the last. The dip tube is the plastic tube that runs from the valve to the bottom of the can, carrying liquid product up to the valve under propellant pressure. Its length, bore, material and end cut control residual product, spray rate and compatibility with the formulation, which is why it deserves deliberate specification and not a catalogue default. This guide explains how a dip tube works, how length and positioning decide product depletion, how bore size and the vapor tap shape spray consistency, how to match tube material to the formula, and how to test a design before scale-up. It extends the pressure and valve fundamentals in our How Aerosol Cans Work guide and sits within the wider aerosol technology and formulations knowledge base.

How an Aerosol Dip Tube Works

In a standard upright aerosol, the valve sits at the top of the can while the liquid product collects at the bottom, so something has to bridge the gap. The dip tube does that job. When the actuator opens the valve, propellant pressure pushes the liquid up the tube, through the valve body and out of the orifices as a spray. Because the tube is the first restriction in the flow path, its design shapes everything that happens downstream of it.

  • Dip tube: carries liquid from the base of the can to the valve; its length sets where liquid is drawn from and its bore sets the first flow resistance
  • Valve body and stem orifices: meter the flow and begin atomisation as the product leaves the valve
  • Actuator: shapes the final spray pattern once the product exits the stem
  • Propellant: supplies the pressure that drives liquid up the tube

These parts act as a series of restrictions, so the dip tube cannot be specified in isolation. Reducing the bore of the dip tube, like reducing the body or stem orifice, generally lowers the spray rate and the particle size and can increase the amount of product retained in the can. The tube also connects to the valve body, and the elastomer gasket materials that govern long-term sealing against the formulation are covered in our aerosol valve gasket materials guide.

Not every aerosol uses a dip tube. Bag-on-valve systems hold the product in a sealed, laminated pouch separated from the propellant, which removes the dip tube and allows dispensing in any orientation. That design is explored in our bag-on-valve technology guide and summarised in the Wikipedia aerosol spray overview.

Dip Tube Length and Positioning: Controlling Product Depletion

Dip tube length decides how much liquid is left in the can when spraying stops. A tube that reaches close to the base of the can draws product down to a small residue, while a tube that stops short leaves liquid beneath its inlet that the user can never spray. Length therefore has to be matched to the internal height of the container and the installed height of the valve assembly, because tube, valve and can all vary slightly from part to part. A correctly sized tube allows containers to be substantially emptied despite that manufacturing variation.

Tube conditionEffect on depletionEffect on spray
Too shortLiquid remains beneath the tube inlet, so residual product is higherSpray can turn gassy while product is still in the can
Matched to container heightThe inlet reaches the lowest liquid, so residue is minimalLiquid is drawn consistently until the can is nearly empty
Too longThe tip can seat flat against the can base and restrict the inletThe draw can be reduced or interrupted

End geometry matters as much as length. Dip tubes are commonly supplied with a straight or an angled cut, and an angled end keeps an entry path for liquid open if the tip meets the base of the can. Tube stiffness also affects positioning, because a flexible tube can curl as it is inserted through the can while a stiffer tube holds a straighter line to the base. Assembly faults of this kind sit alongside the valve and seal failures catalogued in our why aerosol cans leak guide.

Rule of Thumb: Specify dip tube length from measured, filled-can geometry rather than a catalogue default: take the installed valve height and the internal can depth from crimped production cans, then confirm the length with a depletion test.

Bore Size, Vapor Taps and Aerosol Spray Consistency

Spray consistency means the last sprays from a can look and feel like the first. Three dip tube features influence it: the internal bore of the tube, the viscosity of the product it has to carry, and the presence of a vapor tap. The bore sets the flow resistance of the first stage of the flow path, so it has to be balanced against the body and stem orifices and not chosen alone.

A vapor tap is a small hole in the dip tube or valve body that lets propellant vapor mix with the liquid on its way to the valve. In the valve patent literature the hole sits in the tube above the liquid entrance and below the initial liquid level, so that once the liquid level falls below it, vapor enters the tube and mixes with the remaining liquid, substantially re-establishing the spray characteristics that would otherwise deteriorate late in the can's life. The hole is smaller than both the exit orifice and the bore of the tube, and the ratio of these cross-sectional areas is critical to how well it works.

Design featureWhat it changesDesign consideration
Dip tube boreSets first-stage flow resistance; a smaller bore generally lowers spray rate and particle size and can increase retained productBalance against the body and stem orifices; never size it alone
Product viscosityA more viscous product meets higher resistance in a narrow tubeThe flow path must not choke the draw of a thick formulation
Vapor tap holeLets propellant vapor mix with liquid, restoring spray characteristics once the level falls below the holeSmaller than the exit orifice and the tube bore; the area ratio is critical
Valve architectureChanges the downstream path the liquid takes after the tubeReview alongside tilt valve versus vertical valve selection

The effect of bore on flow follows the same fluid mechanics that govern flow through any narrow tube: resistance rises as the bore narrows and as the fluid becomes more viscous, as described by the Hagen–Poiseuille equation. For a dip tube this means a thick formulation and a narrow bore compound each other, and the combination shows up as a weak or uneven spray.

Key Insight: Spray inconsistency late in a can's life can originate in the dip tube and valve path as well as in the formulation, so check tube length, bore and vapor tap before reformulating.

Dip Tube Material and Chemical Compatibility

Dip tubes are commonly extruded from polyethylene in LDPE, MDPE or HDPE grades, or from polypropylene, and the choice is driven by compatibility with the formulation. The tube sits in constant contact with solvents, actives and liquefied propellant for the full shelf life, so a mismatch shows up as swelling, softening, stiffening or cracking. Patent literature on dip tubes notes that environmental-stress-cracking-resistant grades of polypropylene or HDPE matter where the product is prone to causing that failure, and that the resistance is unnecessary where no such substance is present.

MaterialCharacterCompatibility consideration
Polyethylene (LDPE, MDPE, HDPE)Flexible and widely extruded for dip tubes; stiffness rises from low- to high-density gradesConfirm resistance to the formulation's solvents, since solvent absorption can soften or swell polyolefin tubing
PolypropyleneStiffer than low-density polyethylene and widely used for dip tubesStress-cracking-resistant grades matter where the product is prone to inducing that failure

Compatibility is confirmed by storage testing of the complete assembly, meaning can, valve, gasket, dip tube and formulation together, and not from material data sheets alone. It is the same principle behind the elastomer testing covered in our valve gasket compatibility guide, and it matters most for solvent-rich products that sit in contact with the tube for months.

Testing an Aerosol Dip Tube Design Before Scale-Up

A dip tube specification is only proven when filled cans behave the same way from first spray to last. Testing therefore follows the product through its whole life instead of checking a single spray. The core measures are delivery rate, residual product at end of life and spray quality across the can's contents. ASTM D3069, which ASTM now classes as a historical standard for delivery rate of aerosol products, states that delivery-rate tests help evaluate one aspect of valve performance and are a prerequisite to storage testing.

  1. Fill representative cans with the final formulation and propellant, using production-grade dip tubes, valves and cans
  2. Measure delivery rate at the start, middle and end of the can's life to confirm that the draw stays steady
  3. Spray to exhaustion and weigh the residual product to quantify depletion
  4. Inspect spray quality across the can's life, noting any gassy or spitting discharge near the end
  5. Store the filled cans and repeat the tests, so swelling, softening or stress cracking of the tube is caught before launch
  6. Repeat on production-line cans, because tube length and seating differ between hand-built laboratory cans and line-filled units

Results from this testing belong in the technical file that supports scale-up, alongside the process controls described in our From Lab to Factory guide. Industry bodies such as BAMA publish technical guidance for aerosol manufacturers that can help frame acceptance criteria for delivery rate and residual product.

Troubleshooting Dip Tube Problems and Design Fixes

Dip tube problems show up as a small set of symptoms, and each one points to a specific design variable to check first. Working from symptom to variable shortens troubleshooting and avoids reformulating a product whose real fault is mechanical. The table below maps the most common symptoms to the first variable worth examining.

SymptomFirst design variable to check
Product left in a can that has stopped sprayingTube length against container height, and the end cut
Spray turns gassy or spits late in the can's lifePresence and sizing of a vapor tap, and the tube bore
Spray rate lower than targetTube bore against the body and stem orifices; reducing the bore lowers spray rate
Tube softens, swells or cracks in storageTube material grade against the formulation, including stress-cracking-resistant grades
Weak or no draw on the first actuationsTube fit and seating at the valve body connection

When a product genuinely has to spray from any angle, the fix is architectural and not a longer tube. A bag-on-valve system removes the dip tube altogether, and the trade-offs against a conventional dip-tube can are covered in our bag-on-valve technology guide. For a conventional can, the design discipline is the one running through this guide: specify length from real geometry, balance bore against the valve path, choose material from storage data, and prove the result on production cans.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does an aerosol dip tube do?
The dip tube is the plastic tube that runs from the valve to the bottom of the can. When the valve opens, propellant pressure pushes liquid product up the tube to the valve, so the tube determines where liquid is drawn from and how freely it flows.
How long should an aerosol dip tube be?
A dip tube should reach as close to the base of the can as the container allows without seating flat against it, matched to the internal can height and the installed valve height. An angled end cut keeps an entry path for liquid open, and the final length is confirmed with a depletion test on production cans.
Why does an aerosol spray gassy or spit near the end of the can?
Near the end of the can the liquid level falls to the tube inlet and the tube starts drawing propellant vapor along with the last of the liquid. A vapor tap hole in the tube lets vapor mix with the liquid before it reaches the valve, which substantially re-establishes the spray characteristics late in the can's life.
What materials are aerosol dip tubes made from?
Dip tubes are commonly extruded from polyethylene in LDPE, MDPE or HDPE grades, or from polypropylene. The choice is driven by compatibility with the formulation, and stress-cracking-resistant grades matter where the product is prone to causing that failure.
Does dip tube diameter affect spray rate?
Yes. Reducing the internal diameter of the dip tube, like reducing the body or stem orifice, generally lowers the spray rate and particle size and can increase the amount of product retained in the can. The tube bore therefore has to be balanced against the rest of the valve flow path.
Do all aerosol cans use a dip tube?
No. Bag-on-valve systems hold the product in a sealed laminated pouch separated from the propellant, so they do not use a dip tube and can dispense in any orientation. Conventional upright aerosols rely on a dip tube to reach the liquid at the base of the can.
How is dip tube performance tested?
Dip tube performance is tested by measuring delivery rate at the start, middle and end of the can's life, spraying to exhaustion to weigh residual product, and inspecting spray quality throughout. The tests are repeated after storage and on production-line cans to catch tube swelling and variation in tube length and seating.
